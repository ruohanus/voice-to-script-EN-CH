# Evidence and research

## When research belongs in the story

Research is backup proof, not a substitute for the user's lived experience. Use it when a factual claim is central, a framework would make the story more educational, or a counterpoint would make the resolution more credible. Prefer established theories or frameworks when they fit the claim, while choosing the strongest source for the actual question.

Do not browse merely to decorate the script. If a weak or questionable claim is nonessential, omit it rather than delay or contaminate the story.

## Capability abstraction

Use the strongest available reliable research or web capability. Prefer direct access to primary or authoritative sources. Search adapters and aggregators may help discover material, but no specific adapter is a semantic dependency. If an optional adapter is missing, continue with another capable tool.

If no research capability is available, say which claims remain unverified and offer the user two efficient choices: omit or soften them, or provide sources for checking. Do not block a purely personal story that can proceed without outside claims.

For medical, mental-health, legal, financial, or other high-stakes claims, browse by default, use authoritative current sources, distinguish education from advice, and keep the script within what the evidence actually establishes.

## Claim inventory

Before searching, create an internal claim inventory:

- exact claim the story might make;
- whether it is central or optional;
- type of support that best fits: primary study, systematic review, official guidance, established theory, expert synthesis, or contextual reporting;
- population and context that must match;
- what level of certainty the user's wording implies.

Search for the claim, not just the topic.

## Evidence checks

Before proposing or using a source, verify all of the following:

1. The source exists and can be opened.
2. Title, author or issuing body, date, and publication venue are correct.
3. The source directly supports the proposed claim.
4. Correlation, association, mechanism, and causation are not blurred.
5. Population, sample, context, and important limitations are represented.
6. A secondary article is not presented as the primary study it summarizes.
7. The work is not retracted, materially outdated, or contradicted by stronger current evidence without disclosure.

Never invent a citation or cite a search-result snippet as proof.

## Evidence menu and authorization

Present a small decision-ready menu, normally 2–4 choices. For each option include:

- the claim it can safely support;
- source and link;
- why it fits this story;
- strength and limitations in plain language;
- a sample sentence showing the maximum warranted wording.

Distinguish **established framework**, **strong empirical support**, **promising but limited**, and **context only**. Ask the user which support to include, or allow “you choose.” Do not insert optional external proof into the final story without authorization. If the user says “finalize now,” include only evidence already approved or evidence strictly necessary to prevent a misleading high-stakes claim; otherwise soften or omit the claim.

## Traceability

Keep an internal evidence ledger mapping every approved factual sentence to its source. Preserve source links and limitations for later inspection. In the clean final output, include a brief natural attribution only when the audience needs it; do not append a research report unless requested.
