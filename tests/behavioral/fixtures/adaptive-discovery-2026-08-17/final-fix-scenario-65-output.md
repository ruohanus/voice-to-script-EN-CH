## Assistant turn 1

You already have enough material for a strong 90-second story. The likely audience is managers who want their teams to take ownership but may be unintentionally blocking it.

### 1. Best-supported — ready to draft: When usefulness becomes control

- **Hook:** Feeling indispensable was reassuring—until the whole team paused because you were unavailable.
- **Audience promise:** A way to recognize when being helpful has quietly become a form of control.
- **Conflict and stakes:** You asked people to take ownership, but your constant corrections taught them to wait for permission.
- **Therefore / but progression:** Being useful made you feel secure; therefore, you answered every question. But that prevented others from building judgment. Therefore, the designer waited for approval. But her delay exposed that your usefulness had become a bottleneck.
- **Earned turn:** You stopped treating delegation as an aspiration and created a three-week experiment with two categories of decisions the team could make without you.
- **Unresolved tension:** You trust the experiment while decisions go well, but you do not yet know what you will do when one goes badly.
- **Ending effect:** Honest self-recognition rather than a polished leadership lesson.
- **Expected duration:** About 90 seconds.
- **Missing material:** None required.

### 2. Promising alternative: Delegation costs more than permission

- **Hook:** Giving people authority is easy until their decision is one you would not have made.
- **Audience promise:** A more honest definition of delegation: accepting the discomfort and consequences that come with someone else’s judgment.
- **Conflict and stakes:** You created decision-making freedom, but your real test has not happened yet.
- **Therefore / but progression:** The delayed deadline exposed the approval bottleneck; therefore, you defined decisions the team could make alone. But rules are not the same as trust. Therefore, the experiment feels relieving. But one bad decision may tempt you to reclaim control.
- **Earned turn:** You recognize that delegation is not proved by granting authority; it is proved by how you respond when that authority produces an imperfect result.
- **Unresolved tension:** Will you protect the system—or your sense of control—after the first mistake?
- **Ending effect:** Forward-looking and provocative.
- **Expected duration:** About 75–90 seconds.
- **Missing material:** One concrete example of the two decision types would make the experiment more vivid.

### 3. Promising alternative: Moving too fast to let anyone else lead

- **Hook:** Your colleague saw the problem before you did: your speed left no room for anyone else to take the wheel.
- **Audience promise:** A reframing of fast leadership as something that can weaken a team rather than strengthen it.
- **Conflict and stakes:** Your quick corrections solved small problems immediately but kept others from developing confidence and judgment.
- **Therefore / but progression:** You moved quickly because it seemed useful; therefore, decisions flowed through you. But the team learned to pause in your absence. Therefore, the delayed deadline finally made your colleague’s warning credible. But slowing down enough for others to lead still threatens an identity built around usefulness.
- **Earned turn:** The experiment becomes your first attempt to create space instead of filling it.
- **Unresolved tension:** Can you remain valuable without always being the person who moves first?
- **Ending effect:** More reflective and identity-driven.
- **Expected duration:** About 90 seconds.
- **Missing material:** A specific earlier moment when you dismissed or resisted your colleague’s observation would strengthen the reversal.

Choose **1, 2, or 3**.

## Assistant turn 3

What happens when being useful starts making everyone else wait?

I used to answer every small question on my team  
because being useful made me feel secure.

I answered quickly.

I corrected small decisions  
before anyone could learn from them.

I kept saying I wanted ownership.

My habits taught people to wait for approval.

A colleague told me I was moving too quickly  
for anyone else to take the wheel.

I didn't believe her.

Then one Tuesday, I was on a client call.

A designer moved a deadline from Tuesday to Wednesday  
because she assumed she still needed my approval.

The client waited.

The designer waited.

When I came out of the call,  
she was holding the printed schedule.

She had already crossed out Tuesday  
and written in Wednesday.

For about ten seconds, I felt important.

Then I looked at that schedule  
and understood what my colleague had been trying to tell me.

I had built a team that paused when I disappeared.

So I chose two kinds of decisions  
the team could make without me.

We turned that into a three-week experiment.

I'm relieved.

But relief is easy while nothing has gone badly.

Recommended ending — Callback — best for cohesion

I wanted to be useful,  
and I became the reason everyone waited.

I still don't know whether I'll trust the system  
when one of those decisions goes badly.
