## English

What do you do when your team needs direction,
but the data doesn't agree?

I lead a seven-person product team.
Nine days before a small client pilot,
our support dashboard and product dashboard told different stories.

One said the new onboarding flow looked ready.
The other gave us reasons to hesitate.

In Tuesday's launch review,
I could explain what each dashboard showed.
I couldn't explain which one deserved more weight.

So I stayed quiet.
I thought a leader should speak only when the answer was settled.

But my silence didn't pause the decision.
It made the decision for everyone else.

On Wednesday, an engineer asked whether to tune the alert threshold
or keep testing.
I said I needed more time.

On Thursday, the designer held the onboarding email.
She had taken my silence to mean the launch was blocked.
That's a paraphrase.
I don't remember her exact wording.

Then, on Friday, a teammate said,
"If we wait for perfect clarity, we'll lose another week."

That sentence bothered me.
The pilot was small.
It was reversible.
And I'd treated it as if one wrong move would become permanent.

I wasn't protecting the team from uncertainty.
I was leaving them inside it without direction.

The sentence I wrote for myself was this:
I had confused uncertainty with permission to stay silent.
The next Monday, I opened the planning call
by naming what I knew, what I didn't know,
and the decision I was making for one week.

We would send the pilot to twenty people.
We'd review it after forty-eight hours.
And we'd roll it back
if more than five support tickets pointed to the same failure.

By Wednesday, seventeen people had used it.
Three support tickets came in.
One exposed confusing copy,
and we changed it before inviting anyone else.

That experiment didn't prove speaking early is always wise.
It showed me something narrower.

I could make a bounded decision
without pretending the uncertainty had disappeared.

Now, when the answer isn't settled,
I don't think my job is to manufacture certainty.
My job is to name the limits,
choose the next reversible step,
and give the team a point in time to look again.

## 简体中文

当团队在等你给方向，
可两份数据偏偏对不上，
你会怎么办？

我带一个七人的产品团队。
离一场小规模客户试点还有九天，
客服看板和产品看板给出了不同的答案。

一边看起来已经可以上线，
另一边又让人不敢马上点头。

周二的上线评审会上，
我能解释两块看板各自显示了什么，
却说不清到底该更相信哪一块。

所以我没说话。
那时我觉得，
领导者应该等答案确定了再开口。

可我的沉默并没有让决定暂停。
它只是替其他人做了一个决定。

周三，工程师来问我，
是调整告警阈值，还是继续测试。
我说，我还需要一点时间。

周四，设计师暂缓了新手引导邮件。
她把我的沉默理解成这次上线已经卡住了。
这是转述，
我不记得她当时的原话。

周五，一位同事对我说：
“如果非要等到一切都清楚，我们又要损失一周。”

这句话让我很不舒服。
因为试点本来就很小，
而且随时可以撤回。

我却把它当成了一个不能出错的决定，
好像只要走错一步，后果就会永久留下。

我并没有在替团队挡住不确定性。
我只是让大家留在不确定里，
却没有给方向。

后来，我给自己写了一句话：
我把不确定，误当成了继续沉默的理由。
下一个周一，我一开计划会，
先说清楚我知道什么，
不知道什么，
以及我决定接下来一周怎么做。

我们会把试点发给二十个人。
四十八小时后复盘。
如果超过五张客服工单指向同一个问题，
我们就撤回。

到了周三，十七个人已经用过这个流程。
我们收到了三张客服工单。
其中一张指出文案容易让人困惑，
我们改完后，才继续邀请其他人。

这次试点没有证明，
越早开口就一定越对。
它让我看到的是一件更小、更具体的事。

我可以在边界清楚的情况下做决定，
而不用假装不确定性已经消失。

现在，答案还没定的时候，
我不再觉得自己的任务是制造确定感。
我要说清楚边界，
选一个可以撤回的下一步，
再定好什么时候回来重新看。

我现在知道，不确定还在，我也可以给方向。
但如果代价真的很高，决定也难撤回，我还会不会说得这么清楚？
