The strongest story is already visible: you didn’t merely fail to delegate—you accidentally trained the team to wait for you. The ten seconds of feeling important gives the story its honest sting, while the three-week experiment creates movement without pretending the problem is solved.

Before drafting, choose a duration: an exact length, a rough range, or freestyle. Freestyle follows the story and aims for 2–15 minutes, but it may be around 45 seconds if that is the strongest complete version; it can exceed 15 minutes only after a scope choice, and I won’t pad it. The duration and the amount of available material will determine how many follow-up questions I ask and how deep they go.

Likely audience: managers and team leads who say they want ownership but may still be teaching people to seek permission. You can correct that.

### 1. Best-supported — ready to draft: “I built a team that paused when I disappeared”

- **Hook:** I came out of the client call feeling important for about ten seconds. Then I realized everyone had been waiting for me.
- **Audience promise:** A recognizable way to tell when being “helpful” has quietly made you the bottleneck.
- **Conflict and stakes:** You asked people to take ownership, but repeatedly correcting small decisions taught them that your approval still mattered. The cost became visible when both the client and designer had to wait.
- **Therefore / but progression:** Being useful made you feel secure; therefore, you answered everything. But that prevented others from taking the wheel. A delayed deadline exposed the pattern; therefore, you created two categories of decisions the team could make without you. But the real test will come when they make a bad decision.
- **Earned turn:** Delegation is not giving people permission only when they are likely to be right; it is changing what happens when you are absent.
- **Unresolved tension:** Will you still trust the system when someone’s independent decision goes badly?
- **Ending effect:** Reflective and productively uncomfortable rather than triumphant.
- **Expected duration:** About 2–4 minutes from the current material.
- **Missing material:** None required.

### 2. Promising alternative: “Control can look like helpfulness”

- **Hook:** I never thought of myself as controlling. I thought I was being useful.
- **Audience promise:** Help viewers recognize control when it appears as speed, responsiveness, or high standards.
- **Conflict and stakes:** Your corrections solved small problems quickly, but they also removed the team’s opportunity to develop judgment. What helped today weakened ownership tomorrow.
- **Therefore / but progression:** You could answer quickly; therefore, everyone came to you. But every answer reinforced the habit. A colleague warned that you moved too fast for anyone else to take the wheel; therefore, the delayed deadline became evidence rather than an isolated inconvenience. But one experiment may not undo the pattern.
- **Earned turn:** The question shifts from “Can I make this decision better?” to “What does my intervention teach the team?”
- **Unresolved tension:** How much short-term mess are you genuinely willing to tolerate for long-term ownership?
- **Ending effect:** More practical and challenging, with a sharper leadership takeaway.
- **Expected duration:** About 3–5 minutes with one or two concrete examples.
- **Missing material:** A specific small decision you corrected, what you changed, and what the other person lost the chance to learn.

### 3. Promising alternative: “Who am I if I’m no longer the person everyone needs?”

- **Hook:** Being useful made me feel secure—until I saw what that security was costing the team.
- **Audience promise:** Explore why delegation can threaten a leader’s identity even when they intellectually support it.
- **Conflict and stakes:** You want an independent team, but being needed gives you reassurance and status. Delegation therefore involves surrendering more than tasks.
- **Therefore / but progression:** Answering questions made you feel valuable; therefore, you stayed close to every decision. But the team’s dependence eventually exposed the cost. You created a three-week experiment; therefore, your behavior may change. But the emotional reward of being needed has not disappeared.
- **Earned turn:** Delegation may require finding a sense of value that does not depend on being the answer.
- **Unresolved tension:** When autonomy and your need for security collide again, which one will win?
- **Ending effect:** More intimate and vulnerable, with less emphasis on management technique.
- **Expected duration:** About 3–5 minutes after deeper reflection.
- **Missing material:** What “being useful” protects you from, when you first noticed that identity, and what you hope will replace it.

Choose **1, 2, or 3**, and share your duration preference.
