The seed swap wasn’t a disaster.

So why did feeling unnecessary bother me so much?

We held it.  
People came.  
Seeds changed hands.

But for three weeks before it, I answered almost every question myself.

I approved every table label.  
I checked every change to the signup sheet.

I checked once.  
Then again.  
Then once more.

I cared about getting it right.

But I also liked being the person who knew everything.

I knew which table had the herbs.  
I knew who had promised envelopes.  
I knew whether we had enough jars.

Every question made me feel necessary.

So I kept replying before anyone else could.

There were six other volunteers in the planning chat.

Two of them had run community events before.

I still rewrote the shared instructions on Thursday night.

Friday morning, I messaged everyone to explain the rewrite.

Then I answered questions about the explanation.

I told myself I was helping.

What I was really doing was making every decision pass through me.

Saturday made that impossible to ignore.

We opened at ten.

At 9:40, one volunteer moved the tomato seeds onto two tables.

The line had started blocking the door.

I almost moved the seeds back.

That wasn’t the layout I had approved.

But I left them there.

The line shortened.  
People found the tables.  
Nothing dramatic happened.

Twenty minutes later, someone asked another volunteer where the empty envelopes were.

That volunteer answered without looking for me.

I felt relieved.

Then I felt strangely left out.

That’s the part I don’t enjoy admitting.

Feeling unnecessary bothered me.

I’d said I wanted a shared event.

But I’d built a small system where sharing meant helping me carry out my answers.

I kept calling it care because control sounded uglier.

The next week, we changed three things.

One shared page held the current plan.

Questions went to the group instead of coming straight to me.

Each table had one person who could change its setup that day.

I didn’t suddenly become relaxed.

I checked the page once.  
Then again.

I stopped before once more.

One volunteer changed the herb labels.

I would’ve used different words.

Her labels worked.

Another volunteer noticed that we had too many jars.

They moved half of them to the free table.

I hadn’t planned that.

People took them.

I’m not telling this story because I learned to let go.

That would make the ending cleaner than the experience was.

I still care about the details.

I still like knowing where everything is.

And I still notice the moment when nobody needs my answer.

What changed was smaller.

The tomato tables moved.  
The line shortened.

And I didn’t move them back.
