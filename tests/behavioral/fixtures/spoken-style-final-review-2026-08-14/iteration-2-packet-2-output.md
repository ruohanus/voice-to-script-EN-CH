Last quarter, I had to decide whether to stop reviewing every small decision on my team.

I had become the last checkpoint for documents, customer replies, and routine choices.

I told myself I was protecting quality.

And the risk was real.

Newer people could make mistakes, and some mistakes could cost the team trust.

But there was another part I was less comfortable admitting.

I liked being the person whose opinion settled things.

That kept me at the center of the work.

Over time, the team started waiting.

They waited for my comments on documents.

They waited before sending customer replies.

They waited on choices they could probably have made themselves.

And because they were waiting, I became busier.

From where I sat, that busyness looked like evidence.

It seemed to prove that the team needed me involved in all those decisions.

So I was caught in a system that kept confirming itself.

The more they waited, the busier I became.

The busier I became, the more necessary my review seemed.

A colleague objected when I suggested changing the system.

As I understood her point, newer people could make mistakes that would cost us trust.

She also thought I was moving too quickly from careful review to hands-off management.

I agreed that the risk was real.

I don't remember her exact words, so I won't try to recreate them.

What stayed with me was the question underneath the conversation.

Was I really reducing risk, or was I making myself necessary?

I didn't want to answer that by removing review entirely.

So we made the change smaller.

We chose two kinds of decisions that could move without me.

We wrote down what a good decision looked like.

And we agreed to look at the results after three weeks.

The first week was uncomfortable.

I could see decisions happening without my input.

One person made a choice that wasn't the choice I would have made.

But it was reasonable.

More importantly, that person could explain why they had made it.

The decision wasn't careless.

It was simply different from mine.

Another person brought a harder decision to our regular check-in.

This time, they came with a clearer recommendation than before.

They weren't just waiting for me to supply the answer.

That didn't prove the new system worked.

But it changed what I was paying attention to.

I started looking at whether someone could explain a decision.

I also watched for whether they recognized a harder decision and brought it to the right conversation.

I could see that their waiting made me busier.

And my busyness seemed to confirm that they needed me.

So we let those two kinds of decisions keep moving without my approval.

After three weeks, I still didn't know what would happen over the next year.

I didn't know whether the team would consistently make better decisions.

And I had no proof this approach would work for every team or every kind of decision.

Three weeks was enough to show me something narrower.

Waiting for my approval wasn't just a habit the team had developed.

It was a system I was helping create.

I was protecting quality in some moments.

I was also teaching people that progress depended on my response.

From where I sat, those two things looked almost identical.

I reviewed because I cared.

People waited because my review was required.

Then their waiting made my involvement feel even more important.

I haven't stopped reviewing everything.

I also haven't decided that delegation is always the better answer.

What changed was the size of the question I ask myself.

I stopped framing it as a choice between hands-on and hands-off management.

Now I ask something smaller.

Where am I protecting quality, and where am I teaching people to wait?
