For nearly a year, I kept telling people I was "working on the handbook" for our team.

But most weeks, I did almost anything except write it.

So what did "working on the handbook" mean if I never protected time to write it?

I'd schedule a morning for it.

Then a message would come in, or someone would ask for something.

I'd say yes, move the block, and call that flexibility.

I wrote down the same explanation three different times.

I was busy, so the handbook could wait.

I was useful in the moment, so the handbook could wait.

Urgent things were happening, so the handbook could wait.

By Friday, the handbook could still wait.

The old way I described this was that I was prioritizing the team.

But that wasn't quite true.

The handbook was for the team too.

One Wednesday made the pattern hard to ignore.

I opened the draft at 9:00.

By 9:12, I'd answered a request about a customer question.

By 9:35, I'd joined a call that didn't need me.

At 10:20, I came back to the draft.

I couldn't remember why the second section mattered.

I spent the rest of the hour rereading the first section.

Then I decided it simply wasn't a good writing day.

That sounds minor.

But it happened often enough that new people kept asking the same basic questions.

And the people who already knew the answers became the support system for everyone else.

I kept treating the handbook as something separate from helping the team.

Helping meant answering now.

Writing meant disappearing for a while and hoping nothing went wrong.

So answering felt responsible.

Writing felt selfish.

Even before anyone asked for me, I was preparing to be available.

I can see the mistake more clearly now.

I had confused being easy to reach with making things easier for other people.

So I tried something embarrassingly small: two afternoons with notifications off and one place for questions.

The first afternoon felt selfish.

I kept thinking I should make myself available before anyone even asked.

I didn't become serene.

I didn't suddenly turn into an efficient person.

I just stayed with the awkwardness long enough to finish the section on how we make decisions.

A week later, someone used it without asking me for help.

That was the part that changed how I understood the work.

A quick answer can be useful.

But sometimes the useful thing is work that means the next person doesn't need an answer from me.

I don't think messages are the enemy.

I don't think everyone can protect long blocks of time.

And I still don't have a clean rule for when to respond.

Or when to stay with the slower work.

But now, when I move that block, I try to name the trade I'm making.

I'm not choosing between the team and the handbook.

I'm choosing between helping someone now and making the same question easier next time.
