Was I really prioritizing my team, or just staying easy to reach?

For nearly a year, I told people I was "working on the handbook" for our team.

Most weeks, I did anything except write it.

I would schedule a morning for it.  
Then a message would come in.  
I would say yes, move the block, and call that flexibility.

I had a very reasonable explanation.

I was busy, so the handbook could wait.  
I was useful in the moment, so the handbook could wait.  
I was responding to something urgent, so the handbook could wait.

By Friday, it could still wait.

I used to describe that as prioritizing the team.  
But that isn't quite true.  
The handbook was for the team too.

When you lead a small team, this kind of immediate usefulness can feel very convincing.  
Someone asks, I answer.  
A call appears, I join.  
By the end of the day, I had helped with real things.  
I just hadn't written.

The trouble was that my availability had a cost I wasn't counting.

The clearest day was a Wednesday.

I opened the draft at 9:00.  
By 9:12, I had answered a request about a customer question.  
By 9:35, I had joined a call that didn't need me.

At 10:20, I came back to the draft.  
I couldn't remember why the second section mattered.

So I reread the first section for the rest of the hour.  
Then I declared it wasn't a good writing day.

That sounds minor.  
One customer question.  
One unnecessary call.  
One lost hour.

But it happened often enough that new people kept asking the same basic questions.  
And the people who knew the answers became the support system for everyone else.

I kept saying the handbook could wait because the team needed me.  
But the missing handbook was one reason they kept needing answers.

I kept writing down better explanations for why this happened.

I was busy.  
I was useful.  
I was responding.

Three versions of the same permission to postpone the work.

But understanding my excuse didn't change what I did next.  
I still moved the block.

I knew the handbook mattered.  
What I kept avoiding was being briefly unavailable before anyone had even asked.

I had confused being easy to reach with making things easier for other people.

So I tried something embarrassingly small.

I protected two afternoons.  
I turned off notifications.  
And I gave questions a single place to go.

The first afternoon felt selfish.

I kept thinking I should make myself available before anyone had even asked.

I didn't become serene.  
I didn't become efficient.  
I just stayed with that awkwardness long enough to finish the section on how we make decisions.

A week later, someone used it without asking me for help.

Messages aren't the enemy.  
And I don't think every team leader can protect long blocks of time.

Answering a question can help someone right now.  
Writing down how we make decisions can help someone move without waiting for me.

I had been measuring the first kind because I could feel it immediately.  
The second kind often looked like I wasn't helping anyone.

For a small team, that difference matters.

Being available can keep work moving.  
It can also keep the same questions attached to the same people.

I don't want to disappear when someone genuinely needs me.  
But I also don't want my availability to be the system.
