Last quarter, I had to decide whether to stop reviewing every small decision on my team.

I had become the last checkpoint for documents, customer replies, and routine choices.

Nothing moved until I had looked at it.

I told myself I was protecting quality.

That was partly true.

I also liked being the person whose opinion settled things.

That part was harder to admit.

The team started waiting.

They waited for my comments before sending a document.

They waited for my approval before making a routine choice.

And the more they waited, the busier I became.

Then my busyness seemed to prove that they needed me.

If you’re managing people for the first time, this can feel responsible from the inside.

You’re involved.

You’re careful.

You’re making sure the work is good.

Each review looked reasonable on its own.

One document or customer reply didn’t seem like a system.

Neither did a routine choice.

But together, those choices had made me the final checkpoint.

The team had learned where decisions stopped.

With me.

I could see the loop.

Their waiting created more work for me.

That work made me feel needed.

And because I felt needed, I kept reviewing.

I don’t think the whole thing was about control.

Quality did matter to me.

But control was in there too.

A colleague objected when I suggested changing the system.

As I understood her point, newer people could make mistakes that would cost the team trust.

She also thought I was moving too quickly from careful review to hands-off management.

I agreed that the risk was real.

I don’t remember her exact words, so I won’t pretend I do.

What stayed with me was the question the conversation left behind.

Was I really reducing risk, or was I making myself necessary?

So we chose two kinds of decisions that could move without me.

We wrote down what a good decision looked like.

And we agreed to look at the results after three weeks.

We didn’t remove review entirely.

We made the change small enough that we could watch what happened.

The first week was uncomfortable.

I could see decisions happening without my input.

One choice wasn’t the one I would have made, but it was reasonable.

The person who made it could explain why.

I still preferred my own choice.

But I couldn’t say theirs was careless.

Then someone brought a harder decision to our regular check-in.

They came with a clearer recommendation than before.

After three weeks, I couldn’t claim that the team was making better decisions.

I still didn’t know what would happen over the next year.

And I had no proof that this approach would work everywhere.

But I did know something about the old system.

Waiting for my approval was a system I was helping create.
