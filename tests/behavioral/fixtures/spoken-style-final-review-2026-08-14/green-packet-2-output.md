Last quarter, I had to decide whether to stop reviewing every small decision on my team.

I had become the last checkpoint for documents, customer replies, and routine choices.

Almost everything came through me.

I told myself I was protecting quality.

That was true, at least in part.

But I also liked being the person whose opinion settled things.

That part was harder to admit.

The team started waiting.

They waited for my comments before sending something.

They waited for my approval before making routine choices.

And the more they waited, the busier I became.

Then my busyness seemed to prove that they needed me.

It was a very convenient loop for me.

A colleague objected when I suggested changing the system.

As I understood her point, newer people could make mistakes that would cost the team trust.

She also thought I was moving too quickly from careful review to hands-off management.

I agreed that the risk was real.

I still do.

I don’t remember her exact words.

So I won’t turn that conversation into a neat piece of dialogue.

What I remember is the question it left me with.

Was I reducing risk, or was I making myself necessary?

Those aren’t the same thing.

And I couldn’t answer that question by telling myself I cared about quality.

I had to look at the system I was creating.

I didn’t want to remove review entirely.

That would have ignored the risk my colleague was naming.

But keeping every decision with me was also a decision.

It taught the team to pause.

It taught them that a reasonable choice wasn’t enough until I had touched it.

And it gave me more evidence that I was indispensable.

Waiting for my approval had become a system I was helping create.

So we chose two kinds of decisions that could move without me.

We wrote down what a good decision looked like.

Then we agreed to look at the results after three weeks.

The first week was uncomfortable.

I could see decisions happening without my input.

One choice wasn’t the one I would have made.

But it was reasonable.

The person who made it could explain why.

Another person brought a harder decision to our regular check-in.

This time, they came with a clearer recommendation than before.

After three weeks, I knew the old system more clearly.

The waiting wasn’t just a habit the team had developed.

I was rewarding it every time I made myself the final checkpoint.

Being useful can feel a lot like being necessary.

I still care about quality.

I still think some decisions need review.

But now I have a smaller question when work starts collecting at my desk.

Is this review reducing risk, or is it making me necessary?
