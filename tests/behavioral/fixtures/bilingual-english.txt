Before I planted anything, I thought caring for a garden meant knowing what to do.

Then I planted six tomato seedlings, and a late cold snap killed four of them.

Four out of six.

When you are new, a result like that can start to sound like a verdict. Everyone else seems to understand the garden. You are the one who missed something. You should have known. And if you have to ask for help, maybe that proves you are not ready to be there.

But the four seedlings I lost were not the whole story. There were still two left.

My neighbor Mei showed me how to cover those remaining two with old jars. I followed her advice.

And those two seedlings survived.

The practical lesson was simple: the jars helped me protect the plants. The part I still think about is how I learned to use them. I did not figure it out alone. Mei knew something about caring for plants in that place, and she shared it with me when I needed it.

The cold snap had shown me a gap in my knowledge. Asking Mei did not make that gap disappear. It gave me something useful to do next.

That changed the way I think about care.

I had been treating care like a test. If I knew the answer, I belonged in the garden. If I had to ask, I had failed the test.

The plants did not need me to pass a test. They needed protection. Once I understood that, asking Mei was part of caring for them. It was the next caring thing I could do.

For first-time community-garden volunteers in Vancouver, this may feel familiar. You arrive ready to help, but other people seem more comfortable with the soil, the tools, and the small choices that come up. Their knowledge can look effortless from the outside.

What you cannot see is how often garden knowledge has moved from one person to another. Someone notices. Someone asks. Someone shares what has worked for them. Then the next person knows one more thing than they knew before.

That does not make the loss disappear. I planted six tomato seedlings, and four of them died. I do not want to turn those four into a tidy success story.

But two seedlings were still alive, and I had a choice. I could stay quiet because I was embarrassed by what I did not know, or I could ask someone nearby.

Mei showed me how to cover the remaining two with old jars. I did it, and those two survived.

I was still a beginner. I just was not a beginner trying to learn alone.

So if you are new to a community garden, let yourself ask the basic question. Ask the person in the next plot what they know. Listen to the answer, and use it to care for what is in front of you.

You do not have to know everything before you begin. Asking for local knowledge is part of caring. It is not proof that you do not belong.
